01Motivation

We often learn algorithms by tracing them on paper.

Alice learns Bubble Sort
When we learn an algorithm, we often trace it by hand. Here, Alice is learning Bubble Sort. [1] She starts with 5, 2, 3, 4, 1. She compares 5 and 2, swaps them, then compares 5 and 3, and 5 and 4. (pause) The trace quickly grows longer. Here comes the first challenge: trace explosion. [2] She moves to a new page and copies the current state. But here, she missed a swap, and continues without noticing. [3] To go faster, she starts skipping steps, doing parts of it in her head. [4] Looking back, it's hard to reconstruct the full process. It's the same compare and swap again and again, but nothing on the page shows that structure. That is the second challenge: the abstraction leap. [5] And it's hard to reuse this for other inputs. What she has is a messy record of one specific example. The third challenge is overfitting to one example.
02SketchMind

Supporting algorithmic reasoning through executable sketching

To address these challenges, we present SketchMind. It makes her trace executable: every pen stroke runs an operation and becomes a step. [1] When she notices the same steps repeating, she can merge them into her own reusable pen. [2] To see the pattern behind those steps, she can view the same trace as values, indexes, or parameters. [3] And when she thinks she is done, SketchMind checks her plan by finding a counterexample, an input where it fails. [4] With that, she goes back and refines her plan. Let's see how this works in the system.

The first feature is executable tracing. Each pen is an operation, and each stroke runs it. [1] With the Data pen, she writes 3 and 1, and the ink becomes data: the array 3, 1. [2] With the Compare pen, a stroke under 3 and 1 runs is_bigger of 3 and 1. True. [3] With the Swap pen, an arc over them runs swap, and the numbers actually move. Every stroke becomes a step in her trace.

The second feature lets her turn steps into structure: merge them into conditionals or loops, and reuse the result as a new pen. Her trace so far: is_bigger of 3 and 1 is true, then swap. [1] She drags the swap onto the True branch of is_bigger. Her steps stay as they were; the merge creates a block, compare_and_swap, and gives her a new pen for it. [2] With that pen, her steps on 3, 1, 2 become two compare_and_swaps. [3] Then she drags the second one onto the first. That's a loop, i from 0 to 1: a new block, bubble_up, and a Bubble up pen.

How does SketchMind find it? We use an SMT solver, which finds numbers that satisfy a list of conditions. [1] Next to her plan, we run the standard Bubble Sort. She is missing passes 2 and 3. [2] Each step gives a condition. [3] Her correct pass 1 stays exactly as it is. [4] For the steps she skips, we flip one condition at a time… [5] … [6] …to trigger a step she skipped. [7] Two of these flips conflict with her steps: this one… [8] …and this one. [9] Pass 2 is what she is missing: now Bubble Sort must swap in pass 2. [10] We give the solver these conditions… [11] …and set three goals for a minimal change: fewest numbers changed, then smaller changes, then smaller values. [12] Then we run the model. [13] It finds that changing one number gives three candidates. [14] But smaller changes leave two. [15] Then smaller values pick 3, 1, 7, 2. [16] Her steps on it: the array is not sorted.
03Implementation

Three layers, one loop.

Briefly, the implementation: three layers in one loop. The sketch layer turns handwriting into an array and strokes into operations. Gemini recognizes the digits; for a stroke, what counts is which elements it crosses, not its shape. [1] The execution engine keeps a versioned sequence of steps and replays them, with her own ink. [2] The symbolic engine compiles her trace to SMT-LIB and checks it with Z3. [3] If her plan is correct, she's done. If not, the counterexample comes back as a new instance, and she replays her sketch on it.

We ran a preliminary study with 15 learners and 5 instructors. They first sketched Bubble Sort on paper, then used SketchMind. Then they did the same with Pancake Sort, which was new to them. The pen-and-paper session confirms the challenges we saw: missed drawing errors and omitted steps. [1] Learners also found it hard to draw flips and to find patterns.

Which counterexample makes her missing step easiest to see?

While building SketchMind, we came across some interesting questions. First: is the nearest counterexample the clearest one? Her one pass sorts 3, 1, 7, 4. We look for the smallest change, but "smallest" can be defined in different ways. SketchMind first changes as few positions as possible, then keeps each change small, then prefers smaller numbers. [1] Another option keeps her numbers and only changes their order: swap the positions of 3 and 4. [2] We could also keep all her numbers and add a new one at the end. [3] Which one makes her missing step easiest to see? We think this is worth studying, and a direction to explore.
