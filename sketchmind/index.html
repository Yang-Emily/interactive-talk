Hi everyone, I'm Yang Wu from ETH Zurich. This is joint work with Clément Pit-Claudel at EPFL and April Wang at ETH. Today I'll talk about SketchMind, a system where you build a sorting algorithm by drawing on an execution trace with a pen.
01Motivation

We often learn algorithms by tracing them on paper.

Alice learns Bubble Sort
// Six beats, one per part of the hand-drawn clip (the challenge labels are in the video). When learning algorithms, we often begin with pen-and-paper tracing: manually simulating how data changes step by step. [1] Let's follow Alice, a student learning Bubble Sort. She writes an input: 5, 2, 3, 4, 1. [2] She compares 5 and 2, swaps them, rewrites the array, and continues with the next pair. [3] Challenge 1, trace explosion: the page fills with rewritten arrays, and mistakes slip in, like this drawing error. [4] To cope, she starts skipping steps. [5] Challenge 2, the abstraction leap: she senses she is repeating the same actions, but the page is just rows of numbers without structure. [6] Challenge 3, instance overfitting: after one pass the array may look sorted, so she concludes one pass is enough. That conclusion rests on a single example.

// 21 clicks. For an audience new to SMT. An SMT solver takes facts about unknown numbers and finds numbers that make all of them true, or proves there are none. The facts come from the ground truth. On the left is her plan: three compare_and_swap steps, one pass. We call her input 3, 1, 7, 4: a0, a1, a2, a3. [1] Next to it, naive Bubble Sort on the same example: every pass. Her plan has nothing for passes 2 and 3. [2] Each step gives a fact. Compare 3 and 1: swap, so a0 > a1. [3] 3 and 7, no swap: a0 ≤ a2. [4] 7 and 4, swap: a2 > a3. [5] Pass 2 compares 1 and 3. The 1 started as a1, the 3 as a0: a1 ≤ a0. [6] Then 3 and 4, which started as a0 and a3: a0 ≤ a3. [7] Pass 3 compares 1 and 3 again: a1 ≤ a0. [8] Her plan does pass 1, so we keep those three facts: on the new input her steps behave as before. [9] She skips passes 2 and 3, so we flip their facts, to ask for an input where Bubble Sort does a step she doesn't. Flip a1 ≤ a0… [10] …flip a0 ≤ a3… [11] …and the pass-3 one. [12] a1 > a0 clashes with the kept a0 > a1: impossible. [13] Same for pass 3. [14] Only a0 > a3 works: on the new input Bubble Sort must go through pass 2 and swap at compare_and_swap(1,2), and pass 2 is exactly what her plan is missing. [15] Here is the question in SMT-LIB, the solver's language. Declare four unknown integers, between 1 and 99, all different, and assert the three kept facts plus the flipped one, a0 > a3. [16] Then the goals, in order: change as few of her values as possible; then the smallest total change, the L1 distance; then the smaller numbers. [17] check-sat asks: is there any solution? [18] Her own input can't work, since it breaks the flipped fact, so at least one value must change. With one change, three inputs satisfy every fact: [3, 1, 7, 2], [5, 1, 7, 4] and [6, 1, 7, 4]. [19] The next goal, L1 distance, drops [6, 1, 7, 4]: it moved 3, the others moved 2. [20] Those two are equally close, so the last goal decides: the smaller sum wins, [3, 1, 7, 2]. The solver answers sat. [21] Watch her three steps on it. Compare 3 and 1: swap. 3 and 7: no swap. 7 and 2: swap. She ends with 1, 3, 2, 7: the 3 and the 2 are out of order, not sorted. That's the counterexample from the demo.
03Implementation

Three layers, one loop.

// Three beats. Briefly, the implementation: three layers in one loop. The sketch layer turns handwriting into an array and strokes into operations; Gemini recognizes the digits, and what counts is which elements a stroke crosses, not its shape. [1] The execution engine keeps a versioned sequence of steps and replays them, with her own ink. [2] The symbolic engine compiles her trace to SMT-LIB and checks it with Z3. [3] If her plan is correct, she's done. If not, it generates a counterexample, which comes back as a new instance she replays on her sketch.

06Discussion

Discussion

Is the nearest counterexample the clearest one?
The solver minimizes the change. But the closest failing input may not make the misconception most apparent. Instructors asked for local cues too, e.g. highlighting one operation group.
Sketch first, code later?
Participants suggested a staged pipeline with platforms like “LeetCode”: validate the logic as a sketch, then pay the syntax cost in an IDE.
What would Dijkstra need?
A graph state, distance labels, visited marks, and new primitive pens:
ExtractMinMarkVisitedRelaxEdge+ new SMT predicates
possible design how these pens could run Dijkstra.
Learners already have a visual language.
Learners drew bubbles sized by value, bars, circles, or numbers. Can SketchMind support this expressiveness?
beta Abstract pen: draw anything, then give it a value.
// Three beats. A few things from the discussion that I find interesting. First, the nearest counterexample is not always the clearest one. The solver minimizes the change to her example, but mathematical minimality does not have to match pedagogical clarity. Instructors also asked for more local cues, like highlighting one operation group, without giving away the answer. [1] Sketch first, code later: participants suggested combining it with platforms like LeetCode, validating the logic as a sketch before writing code. [2] Generality: Pancake Sort reused the trace, replay, composition, and validation pipeline, but needed range selection, find-max and flip. Dijkstra would need a graph, distance labels, visited marks, and pens like RelaxEdge, MarkVisited, ExtractMin, plus new SMT predicates. The animation is a possible design: ExtractMin takes the closest unvisited node, MarkVisited marks it, RelaxEdge updates a neighbour's distance; here A drops from 4 to 3 and T from 6 to 4, giving the path S, B, A, T. [3] And learners already bring a rich visual language: one participant drew real bubbles and used their size as the value. Could the system read that? We're trying this in a beta, the Abstract pen: draw any object, then give it a value. Here four bubbles become the array 3, 1, 4, 2, ready to compare and swap.
